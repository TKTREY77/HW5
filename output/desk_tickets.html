1. Agent Performance

How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket and why?

Well in that it assessed the quantitative elements well, understood the constraints, and prioritized effectively. Also, (directionally) instructing the user how to resolve and the order of operations is a good idea, albeit a bit micro-manage-y. Well in that it followed instructions and didn't send messages or pay bills without my sign-off. A good example of a smart call: on ticket 103 the Boss caught that the $264 restock for the 12 missing hoodies was $104 more than the $160 we'd have left after paying invoice #501 and rent, and flagged it for me instead of queuing a purchase.

Poorly in that they went around in circles on every ticket. Every agent re-read the same ticket file even though the Boss passed the facts in the hand-off: get_ticket was called 4 times on 101, 4 times on 102 and 3 times on 103. On ticket 101 they kept circling and confirming size S was 0 (check_restock_path ran twice). Ticket 102 was the worst: it kept confirming the rent when it was already established. check_rent_and_cash ran 3 times and get_board 4 times, for a total of 20 tool calls and about 4.7 minutes just to verify one lease and queue one payment. That's too much. Ticket 103 was the cleanest (14 tool calls) but still re-checked things, but alas, worth checking. The react page is also a little chunky tbh and the UI isn't ideal (a lot of embedded scrolling).

Directionally well. It followed the rules and achieved the desired output, just wasn't that token-efficient in doing so. Across the three tickets it used about 130k tokens (129,601): roughly 45k on 101, 42k on 102 and 42k on 103.

Ticket 101 (~45k tokens, 18 tool calls): it worked to plan. 3. Single Agent vs. Multi-Agent Team

What task(s) would have been simpler to handle as a single agent with tools instead of a multi-agent team, and why?

Well honestly I think having this many agents wasn't helpful in this case. Maybe for a real store or business, but clearly there was too much hand off and cycling that wasn't very token-efficient. Ticket 102 is the clearest example: three agents (Boss → Facilities → Accounting, plus a second Boss → Accounting call), 20 tool calls and about 42k tokens for work one agent could do in 3–4 tool calls (check_rent_and_cash, request_payment_approval, draft_message, update_ticket). Ticket 101 is a close second: a straight chain of three hand-offs where each specialist re-read the same ticket.

For this task, simply assessing dependencies for each task (i.e. timeframes to pay and cash limitations) could be done by one agent and passed to the boss, who could form their response for next steps. Maybe multi-agent would still have been good, but I feel like I had too many agents. I should have specified this from the beginning and put tokenomics in the harness (e.g. routing rules by ticket type, and passing facts in the hand-off so specialists don't re-read the ticket).

4. Solvable New Problems

Describe three new operational problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could successfully solve using the MCP tools we built.

First, phasing of future orders. The agent could be helpful for, say, managing 50 new orders of different timeframes when it's too much for a person to handle. It can project the cash needs and expected timeframes for each in a way that a human can't: check_restock_path and list_vendors give vendor lead times and ETAs (desk date + lead_days), quote_bulk_order gives cost and margin, and get_board shows projected cash after everything pending, exactly like it caught that the 12-hoodie restock didn't fit on ticket 103.

Second, clearly cash management, as a bill and rent calendar: projecting future bills and expected payments, which ones are due or overdue, and the order to pay them without going negative. Tickets 101 and 102 already showed this (overdue invoice #501 plus rent due in 2 days, leaving $160) using list_vendors, check_rent_and_cash, get_board and request_payment_approval, with me signing every payment. Potentially even integrating with QuickBooks or another accounting software so the bills come in automatically.

Finally, (as we learned before) pitching new business to Yale organizations AND doing inventory planning for those orders, like the Yale AI Club request on 103: quote_bulk_order to price a discount above cost, request_price_override for my approval, draft_vendor_order for the restock and draft_message for the pitch (provided the human user reviews and signs off on those orders... see below).

5. Unsolvable New Problems

Describe three new operational problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could not solve with the current tools, explaining the additional tools and agent roles required to solve them.

First, full inventory management not so much. You need real people as things get lost and misplaced. A human would need to update this system, otherwise it might say something is in stock when it's not. Today nothing in our system changes the inventory table at all (even an approved vendor purchase doesn't add hoodies). The addition would be a stock-count / receiving tool that a human stock clerk fills in, so the human verification is the role, not another agent. I ran into this issue frequently in production planning (with tofu) as the total you have never really matches as things get misplaced or go back (food relevant).

Second, I would say things with flexible timeframes... technically rent is due in 2 days (ticket 102)... but is it? if you need to flex cash you can pay late. Will the landlord really kick you out after one late payment. What's the qualitative arrangement you have. You could add payment terms and grace periods to the database, but it's really the social understanding, knowing the consequences with that landlord or vendor, and these are things it can't know for which human intuition is required. I've experienced it.

Third, full automation for order management. You might have heard about the man who went to the AI-powered Taco Bell drive through and ordered 10,000 water cups. A human would know this is a joke, but the AI would have trouble judging if an order is high conviction. Say someone comes in and says they want to order 100 sweaters for a club event, but the human knows the club has poor funding or it would be unrealistic for them to afford this... This is where inventory planning would break down (I know I contradict myself from above slightly). You could add customer-history data and a “gut-check” agent or human reviewer, but adding a human back somewhat defeats the point, since this is a backend system in its essence.
